2.  Related Work

2.1.  ML-Based Reconnaissance and Port-Scan Detection

Recent ML-based approaches for network reconnaissance have reported near-perfect benchmark performance [5, 6], but rarely disaggregate reconnaissance from bulk volumetric attacks or evaluate under temporal holdout [7, 17, 25].

2.2.  Evaluation Leakage in NIDS Datasets

Temporal and flow-level data leakage is well-documented in public NIDS datasets [14, 15, 20]. Random k-fold evaluation inflates F1 by as much as 30% [10, 16, 21]. Our findings extend this to duplicate-row inflation: 54.8% of CIC-IDS-2017 temporal holdout test rows share exact feature vectors with training rows, producing near-perfect F1 on the full test set while the non-overlapping subset shows substantially lower performance.

2.3.  Cross-Dataset Evaluation and Domain Adaptation

Cross-dataset evaluation is uncommon due to feature-schema incompatibilities [1, 22]. Prior work shows that high in-distribution accuracy can collapse under domain shift [8, 9, 11]. This paper extends that observation to a bidirectional design and adds a domain-origin classifier and common-feature ablation to characterise the mechanisms.

2.4.  Anomaly Detection Under Distribution Shift

Unsupervised anomaly detection is often assumed to be more robust to distribution shift than supervised methods [2, 12, 13]. This assumption requires qualification: IF's ranking (ROC-AUC ≥ 0.80) is competitive with supervised models under cross-dataset transfer, but its validation-set-calibrated threshold fails to transfer, producing near-zero F1. The distinction between ranking ability and calibration quality is a primary contribution of this work.

2.5.  Explainability and Statistical Comparison

SHAP attribution for NIDS classifiers typically focuses on in-distribution settings [11, 18, 19]. Formal statistical comparison of classifiers is routinely omitted despite clear guidance [3, 4, 24].

3.3.  Feature Schema

A 20-feature harmonised schema is derived from both datasets: 11 features common to both public datasets (flow-level timing and volume statistics) plus nine host-aggregated features populated from the synthetic corpus and zeroed for the public datasets. All experiments in this paper use the 11-feature primary schema; the 20-feature schema is evaluated in the common-feature ablation (Section 4.6) as a sensitivity analysis. A feature-unit audit revealed that flow_interval_mean is recorded in microseconds in CIC-IDS-2017 and in seconds in UNSW-NB15, a concrete mechanism for the domain separability confirmed by the domain-origin classifier (Section 4.2).

3.4.  Preprocessing

Features are standardised with scikit-learn's StandardScaler, fit exclusively on each condition's training partition (verified against train_baselines.py lines 163–166). Identifiers are dropped before scaling.

3.7.  Metrics and Statistical Framework

F1, MCC, ROC-AUC, and PR-AUC are computed per model, condition, and seed. MCC is reported throughout because F1 under a fixed 0.5 threshold is demonstrably misleading under severe class imbalance (see Sections 4.4 and 5.2). Statistical comparison uses: (i) block bootstrap 95% CIs (duplicate-group-aware resampling, block_size=100, N=1,000, seed 42 predictions); (ii) pairwise McNemar's test with Edwards continuity correction; (iii) Holm–Bonferroni correction across the 10 model pairs per condition; and (iv) Cliff's δ and Vargha–Delaney A as effect sizes. Friedman's test is not applied: the five conditions have fundamentally incomparable test sets (14 to 168,930 rows, 0.33 to 0.94 positive prevalence), making cross-condition rank aggregation invalid.

4.6.  Common-Feature Ablation (11 vs. 20 Features)

The nine host-aggregated features are zero-filled for both public datasets. To test whether this inflation explains the directional asymmetry, we compare V3-11 (primary) against V3-20 (sensitivity) using paired per-seed results. Key findings from the per-seed ablation tables:

Time-wise and CIC→UNSW: V3-11 ≈ V3-20 across all models (max ROC-AUC delta ≤ 0.001 for time-wise; F1 and MCC differences are within seed noise for CIC→UNSW).

UNSW→CIC: MLP is identical across schemas (F1 0.9947/0.9948, MCC 0.918/0.919). RF shows high seed-variance under both schemas (F1 range 0.000–0.946 under V3-11; removing the nine zero columns does not stabilise it). XGBoost is nearly identical (V3-11 ROC-AUC 0.740 vs. V3-20 0.715).

Synthetic-to-real: IF is highly seed-sensitive under V3-11 (F1 0.000–0.995) but collapses consistently under V3-20 (F1 ≈ 0.002 for 4 of 5 seeds). The schema difference affects IF's threshold search on the 86-row training set; no inference about feature importance can be drawn.

The ablation does not support zero-filled host features as the primary driver of the directional asymmetry. The unit-scale mismatch in flow_interval_mean (microseconds vs. seconds), confirmed by the domain-origin classifier, is a more plausible mechanism.

5.  Discussion

5.1.  Generalisation Is Direction-Dependent, Not Architecture-Determined

MLP achieves MCC = 0.918 under UNSW→CIC and MCC = 0.020 under CIC→UNSW — on the same pair of datasets, in the same feature schema, with the same training configuration. The common-feature ablation shows this asymmetry is not primarily explained by zero-filled host features. The domain-origin classifier (ROC-AUC ≈ 1.0, driven by flow_interval_mean unit mismatch) confirms near-perfect dataset separability, which may partially explain why training on UNSW-NB15's second-scale intervals and testing on CIC-IDS-2017's microsecond-scale intervals is qualitatively different from the reverse.

5.2.  Threshold Calibration Is a Primary Variable

Three results in Section 4 show F1 and ROC-AUC pointing in opposite directions: SVM under UNSW→CIC (ROC-AUC = 0.992, F1 ≈ 0); IF under UNSW→CIC (ROC-AUC = 0.838, F1 = 0.067); and IF under synthetic-to-real transfer (ROC-AUC = 0.997, F1 highly variable). In each case, the model's ranking of instances by score is competitive, but the decision threshold calibrated on the source domain does not transfer to the target distribution. This argues for treating threshold calibration as a first-class evaluation variable rather than an implicit fixed hyperparameter, and for reporting ROC-AUC and MCC alongside F1 in every NIDS evaluation involving distributional shift.

5.3.  Isolation Forest: Semi-Supervised, Not Unsupervised

IF in this framework is effectively semi-supervised: score orientation is determined from training labels, and the threshold is selected by validation-set F1 search. Under temporal holdout and synthetic-to-real transfer, this approach works well (ROC-AUC ≥ 0.997). Under cross-dataset transfer, IF achieves competitive ranking (ROC-AUC 0.809–0.838) but its validation-calibrated threshold fails to generalise. This is consistent with IF's density-based score distribution shifting under domain change even when the relative ordering of inlier vs. outlier scores is preserved. The assumption that unsupervised anomaly detection provides inherent domain-shift robustness is not supported by these results in ranking + calibration terms.

5.4.  Duplicate Inflation as a Benchmark Confound

The CIC-IDS-2017 temporal test set is 54.8% duplicate rows. On the clean non-overlapping subset, F1 falls from ≈ 1.0 to 0.729–0.938. The duplicate-sensitivity curve for RF and MLP under UNSW→CIC shows monotone performance increase as duplicates are retained (F1 at cap=1: 0.000 and 0.358 respectively; uncapped: 0.191 and 0.995). These patterns suggest that a significant portion of benchmark performance on this dataset reflects test-set internal repetitiveness rather than out-of-distribution generalisation. Duplicate-corrected evaluation should be a standard requirement for NIDS benchmark reporting.

6.  Threats to Validity

Construct validity: The 11-feature primary schema zeros out nine host-aggregated features for both public datasets; all cross-dataset comparisons operate on an effectively 11-feature representation. Whether host features would provide additional signal is untested.

Internal validity: CIC→UNSW trains on one full dataset and tests on another; it is a dataset-disjoint condition, not a per-host holdout. A genuine host-disjoint experiment on real traffic has not been implemented.

Measurement validity: F1 under a fixed threshold is demonstrably misleading in three conditions in this study (Section 5.2). MCC and ROC-AUC are required to avoid incorrect conclusions.

Statistical conclusion validity: Five seeds measure optimisation variability on fixed training splits; they are not independent replications of the data-collection or labelling process. Bootstrap CIs measure test-set sampling uncertainty for one seed only.

External validity: Both public datasets are 7–9 years old. A third independent, more recent dataset (e.g., BoT-IoT, CSE-CIC-IDS2018) has not been incorporated.

Reproducibility: CIC→UNSW reflects a legacy split configuration not regenerated by the current live build_splits.py; it is recoverable from git history (commit bce655e) but requires separate restoration.

7.  Limitations and Future Work

Extending the duplicate-sensitivity analysis to all five models and both cross-dataset directions.

Investigating threshold calibration strategies (isotonic regression, Platt scaling, prevalence-adjusted decision) as first-class evaluation variables under domain shift.

Applying host-grouped deduplication and host-disjoint split construction to larger, real-traffic datasets with reliable source-IP metadata.

Running a third independent, more recent dataset as an additional transfer condition.

Studying the directional asymmetry mechanism in more depth — specifically whether unit-normalisation of flow_interval_mean before scaling eliminates or reduces it.

9.  Conclusion

This paper presents a reproducible, bidirectional cross-dataset evaluation of five machine-learning classifiers for network reconnaissance detection. The central finding is that cross-dataset generalisation is direction-dependent and cannot be attributed to model architecture or feature schema alone: one model (MLP) achieves MCC = 0.918 in one transfer direction and MCC = 0.020 in the reverse, and a common-feature ablation does not explain the asymmetry. Three concurrent findings qualify how NIDS benchmarks should be interpreted: (1) in-distribution F1 ≈ 1 on CIC-IDS-2017 is substantially inflated by duplicate flows—on the non-overlapping clean subset F1 falls to 0.729–0.938; (2) threshold calibration is a primary determinant of per-class detection ability under domain shift, separate from and not predictable from ranking ability (ROC-AUC); and (3) a domain-origin classifier confirms near-perfect dataset separability driven by a concrete unit-scale mismatch, providing a falsifiable mechanism for the transfer failures. Together these results argue against relying on a single-direction cross-dataset evaluation as evidence of deployment readiness, and for reporting duplicate-corrected MCC and ROC-AUC alongside F1 as a minimum standard for NIDS evaluation under distributional shift.
